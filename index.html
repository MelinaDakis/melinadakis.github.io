Object-oriented thinking

Mapping the concepts behind Nordstrom.com

The exercise: choose a familiar digital product, identify its underlying conceptual objects, and sketch how they relate, the same structural thinking that shapes clear, consistent UX writing across a product.

Twelve concepts identified across Nordstrom.com's shopping and account experience, grouped by relationship strength.

Why it matters for UX writing

Wishlist and Cart look similar since both hold products a shopper is interested in, but they mean different things: Wishlist is for saving, Cart is for buying. That distinction should drive different button text, messaging, and follow-up emails, not just different code paths. The model also separates Address and Payment Method by their real owner, the Customer, rather than the Order, since people save that information long before they ever check out.

Features, epics & constraints

Breaking down Nordstrom checkout

The exercise: identify a feature complex enough to qualify as an epic, break it into user stories, then identify a real constraint and explain how it shapes the design.

Feature as an epic: Checkout

Checkout isn't one step, it's several smaller steps in a row: reviewing the cart, adding an address, choosing a payment method, and confirming the order. That's what makes it an epic. It's too big to be a single user story, but every smaller step works toward the same goal of finishing the purchase.

  • As a customer, I want to check my cart before paying, so I know it's right.
  • As a customer, I want to add a shipping address, so my order goes to the right place.
  • As a customer, I want to pick a shipping speed, so I can choose faster or cheaper.
  • As a customer, I want to enter a payment method, so I can pay.
  • As a customer, I want to use a promo code, so I can save money.
  • As a customer, I want to see a summary before confirming, so I know what I'm paying.
  • As a customer, I want a confirmation after ordering, so I know it worked.

Constraint: mobile screen size

Nordstrom's site has to work on a small phone screen, not just a large one. That's a constraint because it limits how much can be shown at once. It doesn't change what checkout does, only how it has to be laid out.

Design implications

Checkout likely splits into several small screens instead of one long page, buttons need to be bigger for tapping, and some information gets hidden behind a "view details" toggle instead of shown right away.

03
Conceptual modeling

Expanding an airline reservation model

The exercise: write a story of use for a persona reserving seats on an already-booked flight, then use that story to identify a gap in an existing conceptual model.

Story of use

Jordan is flying to a conference and wants to sit with a colleague, Sam. The tickets were booked as a group by Jordan's company travel coordinator, so no seats have been assigned yet. Jordan logs into "Manage My Booking," opens the seat map for the departing flight, skips the extra-legroom seats marked with a fee, and picks a standard aisle seat for both themself and Sam. Jordan repeats the process for the return flight, reviews a summary, and confirms. An updated e-ticket arrives by email.

Seat added to the base model: it belongs to a Flight and gets assigned to a Ticket.

What it meant for the interface
  • PassengerId: unchanged, Jordan's login (last name + booking reference) already covers it.
  • SeatMap: needs to show fee per seat, and ask "who is this seat for?" instead of assuming self-assignment.
  • Checkout: stays an open question, since neither leg in this story incurred a fee.
The gap this surfaced

The original model assumed one passenger picks their own seat. Jordan's story needs the seat map to also ask "who is this seat for?", since Jordan is assigning seats on behalf of a colleague. It's a small addition to the model with a real effect on the interface: a passenger picker next to every open seat.

AI-assisted UX research

Auction bidder trust & checkout friction

The exercise: pose a research question to an AI tool, fact-check its answer, then apply domain expertise to judge what it got right, what it missed, and where it borrowed logic from an adjacent field. Domain: high-value collectibles auctions, drawn from years leading social media at Heritage Auctions.

Research question: what causes hesitation or drop-off for first-time bidders between placing a bid and completing checkout on high-value collectibles, and where could UX reduce that friction?

What the AI returned

A six-point list: registration friction, buyer's-premium fee opacity, bid-format confusion, authenticity trust, payment friction on large sums, and post-win shipping uncertainty. The fee figures checked out against real house data.

Held up under scrutiny
  • Fee opacity & payment friction, matches documented checkout research
  • Bid-format confusion, a genuinely confusing mental model for newcomers

What domain expertise added

The AI treated authenticity as one bullet among six. Inside an auction house, it's closer to the whole conversation: a first-time bidder isn't just anxious about the premium, they're anxious about whether the provenance paperwork means anything if something goes wrong after the gavel drops.

The AI also had nothing to say about what happens after a win, dispute resolution, return windows, insurance timelines, the exact moment a lot of first-timers decide whether to ever bid again.

Reflection

AI tools pattern-match general UX principles well and are much weaker at knowing which principle carries the most weight in a specific, high-trust vertical. That gap is where domain experience earns its keep.

See the full critical evaluation

Thin, generic, or missing

I ran an AI research-question template against a question I actually know the answer to: why do first-time bidders get cold feet somewhere between placing a bid and finishing checkout on high-value collectibles? The AI came back with a solid starter list, fee opacity, payment friction, bid-format confusion, and none of it was wrong. But it read like it had studied e-commerce checkout abandonment and assumed auctions work the same way. They don't, not really.

What it missed is the thing anyone who has actually worked an auction house knows in their bones: authenticity is the whole ballgame. A first-time bidder isn't just nervous about a 20 percent buyer's premium stacking on top of a hammer price they already stretched for. They're nervous about whether that "vintage" watch is actually vintage, whether the grading on the trading card holds up, whether the provenance paperwork means anything if something goes sideways after the gavel drops. The AI treated authenticity as one bullet point among six. In this category, it's closer to the whole conversation, and everything else is secondary anxiety layered on top of that core trust question.

It also had nothing to say about what happens after the win: dispute resolution, return windows if authenticity gets challenged, how long it actually takes to get a high-value item insured and shipped. That's not a footnote. For a lot of first-timers, that's the moment they decide whether to ever bid again.

AI-assisted feature ideation

Drilling into a scheduling tool with AI

The exercise: ask an AI to ideate features for a product, test its answer against real scenarios, verify the gaps, then drill into the strongest ones for interface and use-case detail. Product: a content scheduling tool for social media managers.

Testing the first answer

The AI's initial list covered campaign batching, backup approvers, and performance review cleanly against five real scenarios pulled from hands-on social experience. It said nothing about a mid-week PR crisis or multiple teams needing their own localized variant, both recurring needs, not edge cases, for any brand managing more than one audience.

Verified against real products

A crisis "pause all" control is a headline feature at Hootsuite, Sprinklr, and Agorapulse. Scoped, localized approval chains are the core offering of tools like SOCi. These weren't niche asks, they were standard for the category.

Turning gaps into "how might we"

  • How might we let a manager halt every scheduled post, brand-wide, in seconds during a crisis?
  • How might we let a team or market localize a campaign, including its imagery, without rebuilding the approval chain?

Drilled into both with the same AI session: a structured UML use case and user stories for the crisis control, and a UI pattern (a rollup badge) for flagging localized variants.

The catch the AI wouldn't make itself

A "pause all" control only reaches posts still queued inside the tool. Anything already handed to a platform's own native scheduler isn't reachable by a third-party pause button, a real engineering constraint the AI never flagged until asked directly. The AI is good at describing the well-trodden 80 percent of a feature; the last 20 percent still needed someone who'd lived through both.

See the full prompts & use case

What I asked

"For the crisis pause control, how might this actually appear in the interface, and what interaction details would a social media manager need?"
"Write a UML use case for 'pause all scheduled posts during a crisis,' plus a user story for the social media manager and the brand director."
"What UI pattern would let a brand director's rollup view flag a regional variant that changed more than the standard fields, like swapping the hero image, without making them review every region individually?"

What the AI returned

Use case
Pause All Scheduled Posts During a Crisis
Actor
Social media manager (primary), brand director (secondary)
Precondition
User has publish-level permission; posts are queued across one or more platforms
Main flow
Manager triggers a workspace-level "Pause Publishing" control, confirms, system halts all queued posts, shows a persistent banner, logs the event
Alternate flow
A user without publish permission is redirected to someone who can pause
Postcondition
Nothing publishes until an authorized user explicitly resumes
Strengths and weaknesses of the exchange

The AI was genuinely fast once I named the format I wanted, "a UML use case" got a properly structured artifact on the first try, including an alternate flow I hadn't explicitly asked for. But it stayed idealized until pressed: left alone it described a clean happy path, and only surfaced the badge idea and the permission edge case once I asked pointed follow-up questions. It also missed a real technical constraint I know from experience, a "pause all" control only works for posts still queued inside the tool, until I specifically asked whether it worked across every platform.
